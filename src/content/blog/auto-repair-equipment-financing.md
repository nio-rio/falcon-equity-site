In a repair shop, equipment is capacity. Another lift means another car in progress. A current scan platform means you can diagnose a newer vehicle instead of sending it to the dealer. Calibration equipment means you keep ADAS work in-house instead of subletting it.

All of that costs money up front. **Auto repair equipment financing** lets you add capacity now and pay for it from the work it generates. This guide covers how to decide what to finance, how to structure it and what to avoid.

## Start with billable hours

The question to ask about any piece of equipment isn't "can I get approved?" It's "will this pay for itself?"

For each item, estimate:

1. **What it adds or saves per month.** Extra jobs a new lift lets you book. Sublet fees you stop paying when you calibrate in-house. Comebacks you avoid with better diagnostics. Hours saved by faster equipment.
2. **The monthly payment.** Use the [loan calculator](/resources/loan-calculator) with the quote amount and the term you're considering.
3. **Whether you have the people.** A new bay with no technician to staff it earns nothing.

If the monthly gain clearly beats the payment, it's a sound purchase. If it depends on everything going right, wait or buy smaller. Our [equipment break-even guide](/blog/equipment-financing-break-even) walks through the math in more detail.

## How equipment financing works

Equipment financing is a loan for a specific asset, with the equipment as collateral. You make fixed payments over a set term and own the equipment at the end.

Falcon's [Collateral & Asset Loans](/services/collateral-asset-loans):

- **Who it's for:** businesses buying machinery, vehicles, technology or other equipment
- **Amounts:** $10,000–$1,000,000
- **Terms:** 12–60 months
- **Requirements:** an equipment quote or invoice, 6+ months in business, an active business

## Match the term to the technology

Not all shop equipment ages the same way.

- **Long-life equipment:** lifts, compressors, paint booths, tire machines. These can serve a shop for many years, so a longer term with a lower payment usually makes sense.
- **Fast-changing technology:** diagnostic platforms, ADAS calibration equipment and EV-specific tools. Vehicle technology keeps moving, and software subscriptions and hardware updates may be needed to keep them current. A shorter term avoids paying for equipment after it's been replaced.

Ask the vendor how long the hardware is expected to stay supported and what the ongoing software costs are. Those subscription costs aren't usually part of the financed amount, so add them to your monthly math.

## Get the quote right

The vendor quote is the center of the application. A good one includes:

- Make, model and quantity of each item
- Installation, and any required electrical or concrete work
- Software, licenses and first-year subscriptions
- Training
- Delivery and taxes
- Warranty terms

Ask your specialist which of these can be included. Unexpected installation costs, like reinforcing a floor for a lift, are a common reason shops end up short.

For used equipment, bring the seller's invoice with model, serial number and age. Whether it qualifies depends on the specific unit.

## What not to finance this way

Equipment financing is for equipment. For other shop costs, a different tool usually fits better.

### Parts and slow weeks: a line of credit

Parts are bought before the customer pays, and some weeks are slower than others. A [Business Line of Credit](/services/business-line-of-credit) lets you draw for those gaps and repay as cars go out the door:

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$500,000
- **Terms:** Revolving
- **Requirements:** 6+ months in business, $10K+ monthly revenue, an active bank account

### Fleet and commercial accounts: factoring

If you service fleets or commercial accounts that pay on invoice terms, the shop can end up carrying weeks of parts and labor. The [Invoice Factoring Tool](/services/invoice-factoring-tool) turns those invoices into cash:

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$3,000,000
- **Terms:** Ongoing facility
- **Requirements:** B2B invoices, creditworthy customers, 3+ months in business

Factoring only works on invoices to businesses, not on everyday retail customers who pay at pickup.

## A word on cash advances

A [Merchant Cash Advance](/services/merchant-cash-advance) ($5,000–$5,000,000, 3–18 months, 3+ months in business) can fund quickly, and it's sometimes the only option for a newer shop. But it's an expensive way to buy equipment that will last years. You end up paying off a long-life lift with short, high-cost money. If you must use one, keep it small and short, and read the estimated APR disclosure New York requires on sales-based financing ([23 NYCRR 600.7](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/23-NYCRR-600.7)). Our [factor rate guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-factor-rate) explains how to compare.

If you already carry several advances, [Consolidation of Positions](/services/consolidation-of-positions) can combine them and aims to cut current daily or weekly payments, in some cases by 30%–50%.

## Buying for a second location or a new service line

When a shop opens a second location or adds a new service, like tires, collision, fleet maintenance or EV work, the equipment list gets long fast. Rather than financing each item separately as you go, gather one complete list with quotes and finance the package together over a term that fits the longest-lived items, or split it into two loans: one longer term for durable equipment and one shorter for fast-changing technology. Either way, budget the working capital the new location or line will need during ramp-up, because equipment financing pays for the tools, not for the months before the new bays are full.

## Plan the rollout

Big equipment changes disrupt a shop. A few practical tips:

- **Schedule installation** for your slowest days, and expect some downtime.
- **Train technicians before go-live**, so the new equipment is earning from week one.
- **Market the new capability.** If you can now calibrate ADAS systems or handle EV work, tell your customers and local insurers.
- **Track results.** Compare jobs and hours before and after to confirm the purchase is paying off.

## Preparing your file

Along with the quote, gather:

- Recent business bank statements, complete PDFs
- Owner ID and business details
- A list of any existing equipment notes, leases or advances

Underwriters look closely at deposits, so keep shop revenue in a business account. See [what your bank statements say](/blog/what-your-bank-statements-say) and the full [funding checklist](/blog/24-48-hour-funding-checklist).
