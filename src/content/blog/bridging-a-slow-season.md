A slow season is usually a timing problem, not a profitability problem. The revenue comes back — it just arrives after the bills. The fix is to measure the gap in dollars before it opens, fund it with a product whose repayment matches your season, and treat your savings as a reserve rather than a subsidy.

## Why a slow season breaks otherwise healthy businesses

Fixed costs do not slow down when revenue does. Rent, base payroll, insurance, equipment leases, software and debt payments continue at the same pace whether you are in your busiest month or your quietest.

A restaurant that clears $120,000 in July and $70,000 in February has not become a bad business. It has a cash-flow shape — and most owners manage that shape by reaction, in a predictable order:

1. Stretch payables and negotiate with suppliers.
2. Draw down savings.
3. Put the shortfall on cards or a merchant cash advance taken at the worst possible moment.

Each step is more expensive than the one before it, and step three is usually taken when the bank balance is already thin — which is exactly when underwriting looks least favourably on a file.

## Step one: forecast the gap in dollars

You do not need a financial model. You need thirteen weeks of arithmetic on one page.

- **List weekly inflows** for the next quarter, customer by customer, using realistic collection dates rather than invoice dates.
- **List weekly outflows** on the same rows: payroll dates, rent, taxes, supplier terms, loan and lease payments.
- **Run a cumulative balance** down the right-hand column.

The lowest point in that column is your funding requirement. Not the annual revenue shortfall, not a round number someone suggested — the deepest negative point, plus a buffer for the week your biggest customer pays late.

Three details belong in the forecast from the start: quarterly tax dates, insurance renewals, and any deposit your busy season requires in advance.

## Step two: fund the trough, not the opportunity

Seasonal capital should be sized to the gap. Taking more than the trough requires means paying for money you hold rather than use, and it raises the payment due in your tightest months.

Taking too little is the more common error. If you borrow $8,000 for a $12,000 gap, you will be back in four weeks asking for a second position — and stacking a second payment on top of the first is how a seasonal dip becomes a structural problem.

## Step three: match repayment to how revenue arrives

This is where most seasonal funding goes wrong. A daily-collection product that starts the week you enter your slow season takes cash out of the account on the days you have the least of it. By the time your season returns, you have paid down a large share of the advance but also absorbed the payment at the worst point in the year.

Ask, before you sign:

- **When does the first payment come out?** Can it start after your season turns?
- **Weekly or daily?** Weekly collection leaves more room in the account between payments.
- **What is the term?** A longer term lowers the payment but raises the total cost. For a genuine seasonal gap, a payment sized to your shoulder months is usually worth the extra cost.
- **Is the payment fixed or a percentage of receipts?** Percentage-based collection falls in a slow month by design.
- **Can the schedule be adjusted if the season runs late?** Get the answer in the agreement, not in conversation.

Some programs can be structured around a seasonal pattern; others cannot. Ask specifically, and do not assume.

## Step four: decide what you will not touch

Set your reserve floor before the season starts and write it down. A reserve is not a number you discover when you check the balance — it is one you decided in advance and treat as unavailable.

## Mistakes that turn a dip into a crisis

- **Waiting until the balance is near zero.** Applications look strongest with a healthy balance and no recent overdrafts. Start the conversation two to three months before the gap.
- **Borrowing for growth in a slow season.** Growth capital and bridge capital have different repayment profiles. Mixing them makes it hard to tell which one is working.
- **Stacking to cover the first payment.** A second position loaded on a seasonal gap usually signals the first was mis-sized.

## What lenders look at for a seasonal business

Twelve months of bank statements showing the pattern, and a plain explanation of it. Underwriters are comfortable with seasonality — they are uncomfortable with an unexplained dip. A short written note covering your season dates, why revenue returns, and how you funded the previous year does a lot of work in a file.
