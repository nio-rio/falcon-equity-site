Real estate is one of the few industries where a profitable business can be cash-poor most of the year. Brokerages get paid at closing. Investors get paid at sale or lease-up. Property managers pay vendors before owners reimburse them. In between, the bills keep coming.

A **business line of credit for real estate investors** and real estate firms can cover those gaps. But it's often misunderstood as a way to buy property, which it isn't. This guide explains where it fits.

## First, what a line of credit is for

A business line of credit is working capital for the *business*: the brokerage, the property management company or the operating company behind an investor's portfolio. It's sized to the business's revenue, not to a property's value.

Property purchases and other asset-backed needs are a different product. Falcon offers [real-estate and collateral secured business loans](/services/collateral-asset-loans) through Collateral & Asset Loans — typically about two weeks for real estate, with amounts from $10,000 to $1,000,000 and terms of 12 to 60 months. A line of credit covers the operating gaps around those deals.

Keeping that distinction clear saves time. If you need capital secured by real estate or other business assets, ask about Collateral & Asset Loans. If you need to keep the business moving while deals close, read on.

## How a business line of credit works

A line of credit gives you a limit you can draw on as needed. You repay what you draw, and the limit becomes available again. Falcon's [Business Line of Credit](/services/business-line-of-credit):

- **Who it's for:** businesses that need flexible, on-demand working capital
- **Amounts:** $10,000–$500,000
- **Terms:** Revolving
- **Requirements:** 6+ months in business, $10K+ monthly revenue, an active bank account

## Where it fits for investors

### Rehab overruns

Scope grows. The contractor opens a wall and finds something. The project budget assumed one number and the real number is higher. A short draw covers the overrun so the job doesn't stall, and you repay it from the sale or refinance.

### Carrying costs during vacancy or listing

Property taxes, insurance, utilities and loan payments don't stop while a unit sits empty or a property is on the market. A line can bridge those weeks.

### Timing gaps

Deposits, inspection costs and closing costs sometimes come due before proceeds from another deal arrive. Short, planned draws can line up the timing.

## Where it fits for brokerages and agents

Brokerages carry rent, staff, marketing and agent support every month, but income arrives in lumps at closing. A slow month, or a few deals pushed past month-end, can create a gap even when the pipeline is healthy. A line lets you cover that and repay as closings fund.

It also helps with growth costs that come before revenue: recruiting agents, listing marketing, software and a new office.

## Where it fits for property managers

Property managers often pay for repairs and vendor work before owners reimburse them. When several emergencies hit at once, a line keeps vendors paid and relationships intact.

## Line of credit vs. term loan

For a defined project with a known budget, a [Business Term Loan](/services/business-term-loan) may fit better:

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$2,000,000
- **Terms:** 6–36 months
- **Requirements:** 6+ months in business, $15K+ monthly revenue, 550+ credit score

| | Line of credit | Term loan |
|---|---|---|
| Best for | Unpredictable, repeating gaps | One-time, known costs |
| Draws | As needed | Lump sum |
| Payments | On what you draw | Fixed schedule |
| Example | Rehab overrun, slow closing month | New office build-out, buying a book of management contracts |

Many firms have both: a term loan for the project, a line for the surprises.

## What underwriters look at

For a business line, underwriters focus on the operating business, mostly through its bank account:

- **Consistent deposits.** Rents, commissions, management fees and sale proceeds flowing into a business account.
- **Time in business.** At least six months for Falcon's line.
- **Existing obligations.** Other advances or loans with daily or weekly payments.

The biggest problem we see in real estate files is money mixed between personal and business accounts. Underwriters can only credit revenue they can see in the business account. Read [what your bank statements say](/blog/what-your-bank-statements-say) before applying.

## Questions to ask before you draw

Before each draw, ask three things. What exactly is this for? When will the money come back, and from which deal or payment? What happens if that date slips by a month? If you can answer all three, the draw is a bridge. If you can't answer the second one, it's a hole, and a line of credit is an expensive way to fill a hole. That discipline also shows up in your statements over time, which helps when you ask for a larger limit.

## A note on SBA loans

Investors often ask about SBA loans because the rates can be lower. Be aware that SBA rules exclude passive businesses owned by developers and landlords that don't actively use the property, with limited exceptions ([13 CFR 120.110](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/13/120.110)). Operating businesses like brokerages and property managers may qualify, but rental-holding entities often won't. Check with an SBA lender for your specific structure.

## Use the line, don't live on it

A line of credit works best for short gaps with a known end: a closing date, a lease-up, an owner reimbursement. It works badly as a permanent extension of your budget. Some habits that keep it healthy:

- **Name the repayment event** before every draw.
- **Pay it down to zero** periodically if you can, so the line stays available for real surprises.
- **Don't stack short-term advances on top.** If you already have, [Consolidation of Positions](/services/consolidation-of-positions) can combine existing advances and aims to cut daily or weekly payments, in some cases by 30%–50%.

## Build a simple cash calendar

The most useful tool for a real estate business isn't a loan at all. It's a calendar of when money is expected in and when it's due out: closings, rent rolls, tax and insurance dates, payroll and contractor draws. Lay the next three to six months out on one page. The gaps show you exactly when you'll draw and how much, and the expected closings show you when you'll repay. That plan is also what makes a specialist's job easier, because you can explain each draw before you take it.
