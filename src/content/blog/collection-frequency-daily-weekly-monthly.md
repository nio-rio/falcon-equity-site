Collection frequency does not change what you repay on a merchant cash advance. A $30,000 advance at a 1.30 factor costs $39,000 whether it is collected daily, weekly or monthly. What frequency changes is the number of separate withdrawals, the size of each one, and how much room you have when takings dip for a fortnight.

Those are the things that decide whether a payment schedule is comfortable or corrosive. So it is worth understanding precisely, because the sales conversation usually starts in the wrong place — with the payment amount, as though a smaller number were automatically a better one.

## What frequency does not change

Start with the part that is fixed. All three schedules below repay the same $39,000.

| | Daily collection | Weekly collection | Monthly collection |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Advance | $30,000 | $30,000 | $30,000 |
| Factor rate | 1.30 | 1.30 | 1.30 |
| Total repayment | $39,000 | $39,000 | $39,000 |
| Cost of capital | $9,000 | $9,000 | $9,000 |
| Collection dates (over ~6 months) | ~132 business days | 26 weeks | 6 months |
| Payment per date | about $295 | $1,500 | $6,500 |
| Withdrawals in a normal month | about 22 | 4 or 5 | 1 |

The factor rate sets the total. The schedule only divides it. Any offer that appears cheaper at a different frequency is either a different total, a different term, or a different fee list — not a cheaper way of collecting the same thing.

## What frequency does change

Three things, and they matter more than the payment amount.

**How many chances there are for a pull to fail.** Twenty-two withdrawals a month is twenty-two opportunities for a payment to land on a day the account is short. One withdrawal a month is one. That cuts both ways: a failed daily pull is small and usually recoverable, while a failed monthly pull is a much bigger hole and a much worse conversation.

**How deep any single pull cuts.** $295 out of a Tuesday balance is a detail. $6,500 out of the same account is the difference between making payroll and not.

**How the schedule meets your revenue.** This is the part that actually decides the fit. Two businesses repaying the same $39,000 can have completely different experiences depending on whether money arrives every afternoon by card or once a month on a retainer.

Read honestly, that chart does not say one schedule wins. It says the single monthly pull is the deepest hole — it took that account below zero by day 7 while the daily schedule never fell below about $2,900 — and the daily schedule is the busiest, with 22 draws that leave the account permanently slimmer than its headline balance suggests. Weekly sits between them.

A business with steady card takings can carry a daily pull easily. A business paid on retainer, or on net-30 invoices, often cannot: the money arrives mid-month, but the collection does not wait for it.

## Matching the schedule to how your money lands

| If your money arrives… | Collection that usually fits | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Every day, in card or cash takings | Daily (business days) | The pull tracks the takings, so the proportion stays roughly steady |
| Weekly, with payroll cycles | Weekly | Same rhythm as the money, one decision per week |
| Monthly, on retainers or contracts | Monthly | Fewer, larger pulls instead of 22 small ones against an empty account |
| Seasonally, in bursts | Percentage of receipts, or a reconciliation clause | The payment falls in a slow month instead of draining the buffer |
| In large, irregular invoices | Weekly, with a buffer | Monthly lumps and net-45 customers do not line up |

If your agreement offers a percentage-of-receipts structure or a periodic reconciliation, that is usually the closest fit for a business whose revenue moves — it converts a fixed obligation into one that follows the takings. Ask for it explicitly, because it is not always offered unless requested.

## The four questions to ask in writing

Before you sign, get answers to these in the documents rather than on a call:

1. **Can the collection amount re-set if revenue falls?** Look for a reconciliation or true-up clause, how often it runs, and what evidence you have to supply.
2. **What does a missed pull trigger?** A returned-payment fee is one thing. An event of default that accelerates the balance is another. Read the paragraph, not the summary.
3. **Is there a reconciliation step at all?** If the amount is fixed for the whole term, understand that a slow month does not reduce it.
4. **Can the frequency change later, and what does it cost?** Some agreements allow it without re-underwriting; others treat it as a new deal.

Two follow-ups worth adding, because they come up later rather than at signing: whether an early payoff is credited against the total, and how much notice you get before a pull increases.
