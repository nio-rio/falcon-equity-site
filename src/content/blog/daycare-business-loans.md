Running a daycare, preschool, tutoring center or private school is a business with two calendars. One is the enrollment calendar, with families joining in fall, leaving in summer and paying tuition on their schedule. The other is the cost calendar, with rent, payroll, insurance and licensing every single month.

When those calendars don't line up, or when you want to grow, you need financing. This guide explains how **daycare business loans** and other education business funding work, what lenders look for and how to choose.

## Why education businesses need funding

- **Uneven tuition timing.** Many programs see enrollment and payments shift with the school year, while costs stay steady through summer and holidays.
- **Spending before enrollment.** Opening a new room often means renovating to licensing standards and hiring qualified staff before new families start.
- **Staffing ratios.** In childcare, more children usually means more staff, hired in advance.
- **Vehicles and equipment.** Vans for pickups and field trips, playground equipment, furniture and classroom technology.
- **Subsidy and reimbursement timing.** Centers that accept public childcare subsidies or food program reimbursements may wait on those payments.

## What lenders look at

### Deposits and enrollment stability

Your business bank statements are the center of the file. Underwriters look for steady tuition and fee deposits and how much they swing through the year. If your program is seasonal, explain the pattern up front, ideally with enrollment numbers by month.

### Time in business

Falcon's line of credit, term loan and equipment financing all start at 6+ months in business. A brand-new center will have fewer options.

### Licensing and good standing

Lenders want to know the business can legally operate and grow. Have your current license and any recent inspection reports available.

### Existing obligations

Any loans or advances with frequent payments will show on your statements. Several at once is a concern. [Consolidation of Positions](/services/consolidation-of-positions) can combine existing advances and aims to cut current daily or weekly payments, in some cases by 30%–50%.

### Credit

For a [Business Term Loan](/services/business-term-loan), Falcon requires a 550+ credit score. If your credit needs work, see our [credit repair and loan prep](/resources/credit-repair-loan-prep) resource.

## Which product fits which need

### Summer and holiday gaps: line of credit

A [Business Line of Credit](/services/business-line-of-credit) gives you a revolving limit to draw on in slower months and repay when enrollment picks up:

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$500,000
- **Terms:** Revolving
- **Requirements:** 6+ months in business, $10K+ monthly revenue, an active bank account

### Build-outs and expansion: term loan

Renovating a room, opening a second location or adding a program like an infant room are one-time projects with known costs. A [Business Term Loan](/services/business-term-loan) gives you a lump sum and a fixed payment:

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$2,000,000
- **Terms:** 6–36 months
- **Requirements:** 6+ months in business, $15K+ monthly revenue, 550+ credit score

Make sure the payment works in your lowest-enrollment month.

### Vans, playgrounds and technology: equipment financing

[Collateral & Asset Loans](/services/collateral-asset-loans) finance specific equipment over its working life:

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$1,000,000
- **Terms:** 12–60 months
- **Requirements:** an equipment quote or invoice, 6+ months in business, an active business

Get an itemized quote, including installation and safety surfacing for playground equipment.

### Fast but expensive: merchant cash advance

A [Merchant Cash Advance](/services/merchant-cash-advance) ($5,000–$5,000,000, 3–18 months, 3+ months in business) funds quickly and is easier to qualify for, but it costs considerably more. For a business with thin margins and seasonal dips, frequent repayments can be hard to sustain. Use it only for a short, specific need you can repay quickly. Our [factor rate guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-factor-rate) explains the cost.

## How SBA loans compare

SBA-backed loans can offer lower rates and longer terms, which is attractive for large projects like buying a building. They usually take longer and require more paperwork. They also exclude nonprofit organizations ([13 CFR 120.110](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/13/120.110)), although for-profit subsidiaries can be eligible. The [SBA's loan page](https://www.sba.gov/loans/) explains the programs.

A practical approach for many centers: use an SBA loan for large, long-term projects when timing allows, and a line of credit or term loan for faster or smaller needs.

## For New York City operators

New York City's [Small Business Services](https://nyc-business.nyc.gov/nycbusiness/business-services/financial-assistance/financing-assistance) offers free one-on-one help comparing financing options, including public programs. It's worth checking alongside private financing, especially for larger projects.

## How to strengthen your application

- **Run everything through one business account**: tuition, registration fees, subsidy payments and expenses.
- **Track enrollment by month** and bring a simple summary, including any waitlist.
- **Have your license and inspection history** on hand.
- **Prepare a budget for the project**, with quotes for renovation or equipment.
- **Show how the new capacity pays for itself**: expected new enrollments, tuition and added staff costs.
- **Apply before peak need.** A line approved in spring is ready for summer.

See the [funding checklist](/blog/24-48-hour-funding-checklist) for the standard documents and [what your bank statements say](/blog/what-your-bank-statements-say) for how underwriters read them.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Borrowing for expansion with a seasonal line.** A revolving line is for gaps that close. A renovation you'll pay off over years belongs in a term loan.
- **Underestimating ramp-up.** New rooms rarely fill on day one. Budget for staff and rent during the months it takes to reach full enrollment.
- **Mixing subsidy timing into the plan as if it were tuition.** If public payments arrive on a delay, treat them as receivables and plan for the wait.
- **Stacking short-term advances.** Each one adds a frequent payment from the same tuition deposits.

## Run the numbers before you expand

Expansion is where most education businesses need the biggest amounts, and where the risk is highest. Before committing:

1. Estimate the full cost: renovation, licensing, furniture, staff hired before opening.
2. Estimate how fast new spots will fill, conservatively.
3. Run the payment in the [loan calculator](/resources/loan-calculator).
4. Check that existing revenue can carry the payment until the new room or location fills.
