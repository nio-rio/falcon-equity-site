Running an online store means paying for everything up front. The factory wants a deposit, then the balance before shipment. Freight and duties come next. Ads run before the first order lands. And when sales do come in, the marketplace or payment processor pays you on its own schedule.

**E-commerce inventory financing** bridges that gap. Done well, it keeps your best sellers in stock and lets you grow faster than cash alone would allow. Done badly, it turns a good product into a loss. Here's how to tell the difference.

## The numbers that matter first

Before comparing lenders, work out three numbers for the inventory you want to fund:

1. **Contribution margin per unit.** Sale price minus product cost, freight, duties, platform fees, fulfillment and the ad cost to sell it. This is what's left to pay for financing and overhead.
2. **Cash conversion time.** From the day you pay the supplier to the day the payout for the last unit hits your account. Include production, shipping, receiving, selling time and payout delay.
3. **Sell-through confidence.** How sure are you about the sales rate? A proven reorder is different from a new product.

Financing costs money for as long as it's outstanding. The longer your cash conversion time and the thinner the margin, the less room there is for expensive capital.

## Option 1: Business line of credit

A revolving line works well for sellers who reorder on a cycle. Draw for each purchase order, repay as payouts arrive and draw again.

Falcon's [Business Line of Credit](/services/business-line-of-credit):

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$500,000
- **Terms:** Revolving
- **Requirements:** 6+ months in business, $10K+ monthly revenue, an active bank account

Because you only pay for what you've drawn and only for as long as it's out, a line is usually the most cost-efficient choice for steady replenishment.

## Option 2: Sales-based advance

A [Merchant Cash Advance](/services/merchant-cash-advance) gives you a lump sum and takes a share of future sales until a fixed total is repaid:

- **Amounts:** $5,000–$5,000,000
- **Terms:** 3–18 months
- **Requirements:** 3+ months in business, $10K+ monthly revenue, an active business bank account

The appeal is speed and flexibility. Repayment rises with strong sales and falls when sales slow. For a proven SKU that's about to stock out, that can be worth paying for.

The honest caveat: **it's expensive.** Advances are priced with a factor rate rather than an interest rate, and the annualized cost is typically much higher than a line or a term loan. New York requires providers of sales-based financing to disclose an estimated APR with the offer ([23 NYCRR 600.7](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/23-NYCRR-600.7)), which helps you compare. See our guide on [how to read a factor rate](/blog/how-to-read-a-factor-rate).

## Option 3: Business term loan

For a one-time investment like launching a new product line, moving to a new 3PL or buying a large opening order for a new channel, a [Business Term Loan](/services/business-term-loan) gives you a lump sum and a fixed payment:

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$2,000,000
- **Terms:** 6–36 months
- **Requirements:** 6+ months in business, $15K+ monthly revenue, 550+ credit score

Make sure the payment works in your slowest month, not just after a strong quarter.

## How to compare cost against margin

A simple test works across all three options:

1. Estimate **total financing cost** for the amount and the time you'll have it. For a line, that's what you pay while drawn. For an advance, it's the total payback minus the amount received. For a term loan, it's total interest and fees. The [loan calculator](/resources/business-loan-calculator) can help.
2. Estimate **total contribution profit** from the units you're funding.
3. If profit is comfortably larger than cost, even if the stock sells slower than planned, the financing is working for you. If it only works in the best case, order less or use cheaper money.

This is the same logic whether you sell on your own site, on marketplaces or wholesale.

## Plan for what can go wrong

- **Slow sellers.** Decide in advance at what point you'll discount to free up cash.
- **Payout holds or reserves.** Platforms can delay payouts. Keep a buffer so a hold doesn't cause a missed payment.
- **Supplier delays.** A late shipment shortens your selling window but not your repayment schedule.
- **Ad costs rising.** If acquisition costs jump, your margin shrinks. Revisit the math before reordering.
- **Stacking.** Taking a second advance to cover the first is the fastest way into trouble. If you're already there, [Consolidation of Positions](/services/consolidation-of-positions) can combine existing advances and aims to cut daily or weekly payments, in some cases by 30%–50%.

## Q4 is a special case

Holiday demand means the biggest purchase orders of the year often land at the same time cash is tightest. If you're planning for Q4:

- **Apply for a line well before you need it**, ideally in summer.
- **Split orders** into an initial shipment and a reorder, so you're not funding the whole season at once.
- **Map payout dates** against your repayment schedule through January, when returns come back and sales slow.

## Match the product to the stage of the brand

Early-stage brands with only a few months of sales have fewer choices; Falcon's advance starts at 3+ months in business, while the line of credit and term loan need 6+ months. As sales history grows and deposits become steadier, cheaper options open up. A sensible path for many sellers is to use smaller, short-term funding sparingly while proving the product, then move to a line of credit for regular reorders once the history supports it. Each clean repayment builds the record that makes the next approval easier.

## Preparing your file

Underwriters will mostly look at your business bank statements, where payouts from each platform show up. Helpful to have:

- Recent business bank statements, complete PDFs
- Platform sales reports if your deposits combine several channels
- Owner ID and business details
- A list of any existing advances, loans or platform financing

Keep store revenue flowing into a business account, not a personal one. See [what your bank statements say](/blog/what-your-bank-statements-say).
