A factor rate is a multiplier, not an interest rate. You multiply the amount advanced by the factor to get the total you pay back: **$50,000 at a 1.30 factor means $65,000 repaid**, so the cost is $15,000. An APR is an annual rate, which is why you cannot compare the two without knowing the repayment period — the same factor rate becomes a far higher annualized cost when the money is repaid quickly.

If you want the shorter version of how a quote like 1.30 is built, our guide to [reading a factor rate](/blog/how-to-read-a-factor-rate) covers the basics. This page is about the comparison.

## The arithmetic, in three steps

1. **Turn the factor into a total.** Advance × factor = total payback. $50,000 × 1.30 = $65,000.
2. **Subtract the advance to get the cost.** $65,000 − $50,000 = $15,000.
3. **Annualize that cost against the time you hold the money.** This is the step a factor quote skips, and the one that decides whether an offer is cheap or expensive.

| Factor rate | Payback on a $50,000 advance | Cost in dollars |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1.15 | $57,500 | $7,500 |
| 1.25 | $62,500 | $12,500 |
| 1.30 | $65,000 | $15,000 |
| 1.40 | $70,000 | $20,000 |

## Why the same factor costs wildly different amounts

Take that same $50,000 at a 1.30 factor — $65,000 back, $15,000 of cost — and change only how fast it is repaid. Assuming equal payments across the term, the annualized cost looks like this.

| Repaid over | Cost in dollars | Approximate annualized cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 months | $15,000 | about 400% |
| 6 months | $15,000 | about 153% |
| 12 months | $15,000 | about 65% |
| 18 months | $15,000 | about 41% |

Same fee. Same advance. The annualized cost falls by roughly a factor of ten between a three-month and an eighteen-month repayment, because the money is outstanding for far less time in the first case. A factor rate quoted without a term is half a price.

Two caveats, because they matter. The table assumes equal fixed payments, and many advances remit a percentage of sales instead, so real timing moves with your revenue. And the annualized figure is a comparison tool, not a disclosure: a merchant cash advance is a purchase of future receivables rather than a credit transaction, so it does not carry a regulated APR. Use the figure to rank offers against each other, not as a number anyone is bound to.

## Why paying off early does not save money

With an interest-bearing loan, paying early reduces the interest you would have paid. With a factor-based advance, the total payback is fixed when you sign: the fee is attached to the advance, not to the calendar. Repaying early ends the schedule sooner; it does not normally shrink the fee. Unless your agreement says early payoff reduces the total, assume it does not.

## Comparing two offers fairly

Quotes in different formats are not comparable on their face. Convert both the same way.

- **Total payback in dollars.** Always the first number.
- **Cost as a percentage of the advance.** $15,000 on $50,000 is 30%, which is the factor minus one.
- **The term.** The same cost over six months and over eighteen months are different products.
- **The remittance.** A fixed daily amount and a percentage of daily sales behave differently in a slow month, even at the same total cost.
- **Your worst month.** Run the payment against your slowest month of the last year, not your best.

Falcon merchant cash advances typically run **$5,000 – $5,000,000 over 3 – 18 months** for businesses with 3+ months in operation and $10K+ monthly revenue, with a **0% agreement fee**. Falcon states there are no fees on the agreement value. Whatever a provider calls its charges, ask for the total they will collect and the date the last payment lands. A [business line of credit](/services/business-line-of-credit) works the other way round: you pay only on what you draw, for as long as you draw it.

## The bottom line

Judge an offer by two numbers, not one: the total payback in dollars, and how long you hold the money. A low factor over three months can cost more per year than a higher factor over eighteen, and only the second number tells you which is which.
