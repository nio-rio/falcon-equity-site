A factor rate tells you how much you will repay in total, not how much the money costs per year. Multiply the advance by the factor rate to get the payback amount. To compare that against a loan, convert the total into an annualized rate using the repayment term. Done properly, the comparison is uncomfortable — and useful.

## What a factor rate actually is

A merchant cash advance is priced with a factor rate rather than an interest rate. The factor is a decimal, usually somewhere between 1.1 and 1.5, applied once to the amount advanced.

- Advance: $50,000
- Factor rate: 1.30
- Total repayment: $65,000
- Cost of capital: $15,000

Two things follow from that structure. The cost is fixed at signing, so it does not behave like interest that accrues month by month. And the factor rate on its own tells you nothing about whether the money is expensive, because a 1.30 factor collected over four months is a very different proposition from a 1.30 factor collected over fourteen.

That is why comparing two offers by factor rate alone so often goes wrong.

## Converting a factor rate to an annualized rate

The conversion takes three steps.

1. **Find the cost.** Advance × (factor − 1). For $50,000 at 1.30, that is $50,000 × 0.30 = $15,000.
2. **Express it as a ratio of the advance.** $15,000 ÷ $50,000 = 0.30.
3. **Annualize it.** Ratio × (365 ÷ days in the repayment term).

Run that on the same $50,000 at a 1.30 factor:

| Repayment term | Calculation | Annualized |
|---|---|---|
| 6 months (180 days) | 0.30 × (365 ÷ 180) | about 61% |
| 12 months (365 days) | 0.30 × (365 ÷ 365) | 30% |

Identical factor. More than double the annualized cost, purely because of how long the money is outstanding.

This is a simplified comparison, not a regulated APR disclosure. It is accurate enough to rank competing offers against each other, which is the decision you are actually making.

## The term-length trap

The trap is a low factor stretched over a long collection period. Compare two hypothetical quotes on the same $50,000:

- **Quote A:** 1.25 factor over 4 months (120 days). Cost ratio 0.25. Annualized: 0.25 × (365 ÷ 120) = about 76%.
- **Quote B:** 1.40 factor over 12 months (365 days). Cost ratio 0.40. Annualized: about 40%.

Quote A looks cheaper. It is nearly twice as expensive per year. If you expect the money to be repaid quickly — a short job, a fast-turning inventory buy — the short term can still be the right call. If you are using it to cover a gap that will take a year to close, a high-factor, short-term product is the wrong instrument, even at a lower headline number.

*These figures are illustrative and are not an offer. Actual terms depend on your file.*

## What to ask for in writing

Before you sign anything, get answers to these in the offer documents, not on a call:

- **Total repayment, in dollars.** Not the factor, not the payment — the total.
- **The factor rate and the term used to derive the payment.** You cannot check the arithmetic without both.
- **Payment amount and frequency.** Daily business days, weekly, or a percentage of receipts? A daily collection is not the same cost as the same weekly total.
- **Whether the payment is fixed or moves with revenue.** Fixed payments are predictable but unforgiving in a slow month.
- **Early-payoff terms.** Some agreements credit you for early repayment; others do not. Ask directly.
- **Every fee.** Origination, underwriting, admin, ACH, statement, late. Add them to the cost before you annualize.
- **What the product legally is.** A purchase of future receivables and a loan carry different disclosure requirements, and the APR comparison only runs one way.
- **What happens if revenue falls.** A reconciliation clause, a payment holiday, or nothing at all.

## A short pre-signature checklist

- Recalculate the total repayment from the factor yourself. If your number differs from theirs, stop and ask why.
- Annualize every offer using the same formula, over the term each funder actually quoted.
- Compare against the alternatives you already have: a [line of credit](/services/business-line-of-credit), a [term loan](/services/business-term-loan), a supplier payment plan.
- Check that the payment fits your worst month, not your average one.
- Confirm the amount you are taking matches the use. Extra capital is not free capital.

## The point of the arithmetic

A factor rate is a useful, honest number once you know what it measures. It measures total cost, not annual cost. Convert it, then decide. An offer that survives the conversion is one you can sign with a clear head; an offer that only looks good as a decimal is one to renegotiate or decline.
