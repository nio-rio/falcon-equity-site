Factoring sells individual invoices and pays you within days; receivables financing borrows against your whole ledger, usually as a line you draw from. Factoring suits a business with a few large invoices and an urgent gap. Receivables financing suits a steady book of many smaller invoices and rewards lower cost over speed.

## The gap you are actually trying to close

You did the work in March. Your customer pays in May. In between, you cover payroll, materials and overhead for a job that is already finished and already profitable on paper.

That gap has a cost, and most owners absorb it in the same few ways: a supplier stretches terms and starts charging for it, payroll gets tight in week three, or the next job is delayed because there is no cash to buy materials for it.

## How invoice factoring works

1. You deliver the work and issue an invoice.
2. You send the invoice to the factoring company, which checks the customer's credit and verifies the invoice.
3. The funder advances a percentage of the invoice value.
4. Your customer pays the funder, or pays into an account controlled by the funder.
5. The funder releases the remaining balance, less its fee.

Two features matter more than the mechanics. First, the underwriting is largely on **your customer's** creditworthiness, not only yours — which helps a young or thinly capitalised business with strong clients. Second, notice: some programs require your customer to be told the invoice has been assigned, and some do not. Which one applies changes how the arrangement looks to your client, so ask before you assume.

## How AR financing works

Receivables financing is closer to a line of credit secured by your ledger. You give the lender an aging report, they set a borrowing base against eligible invoices, and you draw what you need against it as invoices are issued and paid.

- The invoices stay yours; you keep collecting them.
- You borrow against the book, not one invoice at a time.
- There is ongoing reporting: an aging report and a borrowing base certificate, usually monthly or more often.
- Setup is heavier, and so is the administration once it is running.

For a business with hundreds of invoices and a credit manager who already tracks them, that administration is routine. For a business with four customers and an owner doing the books at night, it is a burden.

## Side by side

| | Invoice factoring | AR financing |
|---|---|---|
| What is funded | Specific invoices | A borrowing base across the ledger |
| Speed to first funding | Typically faster; individual invoices can be funded as issued | Slower to set up, then available on draw |
| Who collects | Often the funder, sometimes you | You |
| Underwriting focus | The customer's credit | Your ledger quality and your business |
| Ongoing admin | Invoice-by-invoice submission | Aging reports, borrowing base, covenants |
| Best fit | Concentrated, creditworthy customers; urgent gap | Diversified book; recurring need |

## How to choose

Work through these four questions.

**How concentrated is your book?** If two customers are 70% of your receivables, factoring is straightforward — a funder can assess two credits. If you have 200 customers, the concentration advantage disappears and the per-invoice administration becomes the dominant cost.

**How good are your customers' credit profiles?** Factoring leans on them. Weak or slow-paying customers make both products harder, but factoring is the less forgiving of the two.

**How much do you need, and for how long?** A one-off gap against three invoices is a factoring job. A permanent feature of a growing business — receivables always outstanding, always needing to be bridged — is a line.

**Can you live with the customer knowing?** Assignment and notification change the relationship. Some large customers refuse assignment outright, or have contracts that prohibit it. Check the contract before you sign anything with a funder.

## What to have ready

- An accounts receivable aging report, current and clean
- Copies of the invoices themselves, with proof of delivery or acceptance
- Your customer list, with payment history
- Contracts or purchase orders, especially any clause about assignment
- Six months of business bank statements
- A short explanation of your billing cycle and typical payment terms

The aging report is the file. If invoices are disputed, partially delivered, or being paid outside terms, that shows up immediately — and it is better to explain it in advance than to have it discovered.

## Three mistakes worth avoiding

- **Factoring a customer who pays in 90 days.** The cost of the facility runs the whole time the invoice is outstanding. Long payment terms make any receivables product more expensive.
- **Not reading the assignment clause.** Some customer contracts prohibit it. Finding out after you have signed leaves you with a facility you cannot use.
- **Using it to fund losses rather than timing.** Both products bridge a gap between delivery and payment. Neither fixes a job that was priced below cost.
