Agencies, consultancies, law firms, accounting practices, engineering and architecture studios all share the same cash pattern. The biggest cost is people, paid every two weeks. The revenue comes from clients who pay on net terms, at milestones or whenever their accounts payable team gets to it.

A **line of credit for professional services firms** is built for that pattern. But without inventory or equipment to point to, many firm owners aren't sure what lenders will look at. This guide explains how underwriting works for a services business and how to prepare.

## Why services firms need working capital

- **Payroll runs ahead of collections.** You pay staff for the work this month; the client pays for it in a month or two.
- **Engagements end unevenly.** A big project wraps up before the next one starts.
- **You hire ahead of wins.** New people need to be onboarded before the first invoice goes out.
- **Pass-through costs.** Agencies and consultancies may front media, vendors or travel before the client reimburses them.

None of these mean the firm is unprofitable. They mean the timing is uneven, which is exactly what a revolving line is for.

## What underwriters look at in a services firm

### 1. Consistent deposits

Your business bank statements are the core of the file. Underwriters want to see client payments landing regularly and a balance that doesn't hit zero every payroll. Read [what your bank statements say](/blog/what-your-bank-statements-say) for the details.

### 2. Client concentration

This is often the first question for a services firm. If one client is most of your revenue, losing them would change everything, and a lender knows it. A broader client base makes a stronger file. If you're concentrated, explain the relationship: how long it's run, whether there's a contract, and what else is in the pipeline.

### 3. Receivables quality

An **accounts receivable aging report** shows who owes you, how much and for how long. Current invoices to well-known business clients are reassuring. A pile of invoices more than 90 days old is a warning sign. Clean up or write off old balances before you apply.

### 4. Existing obligations

Underwriters will see any daily or weekly payments to other funders. Several short-term advances at once raise concern about cash flow. If that's your situation, [Consolidation of Positions](/services/consolidation-of-positions) can combine them and aims to cut current daily or weekly payments, in some cases by 30%–50%.

### 5. Time in business and credit

Falcon's line asks for 6+ months in business. For a [Business Term Loan](/services/business-term-loan), the requirements are 6+ months, $15K+ monthly revenue and a 550+ credit score. If your credit needs work first, see our [credit repair and loan prep](/resources/credit-repair-loan-prep) resource.

## How to strengthen your file

- **Separate accounts.** Run all client payments and firm expenses through a business account. Revenue that lands in a personal account is hard for an underwriter to credit.
- **Invoice promptly and consistently.** Regular billing creates regular deposits.
- **Avoid overdrafts** in the months before you apply.
- **Prepare a short client summary:** top clients, share of revenue, contract terms and typical payment time.
- **Have an AR aging report ready**, ideally from your accounting software.
- **Apply before you need it.** A line approved in a strong quarter is there when a slow one arrives.

## When factoring is the better fit

If your firm bills large invoices to creditworthy business clients, the [Invoice Factoring Tool](/services/invoice-factoring-tool) may provide more funding than a line:

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$3,000,000
- **Terms:** Ongoing facility
- **Requirements:** B2B invoices, creditworthy customers, 3+ months in business

With factoring, approval leans on your clients' credit, and funding grows as your billings grow. It's common with staffing firms, agencies with large corporate clients and consultancies billing enterprise customers. It's generally not a fit for consumer billing or for fees that depend on an outcome, such as contingency arrangements.

| | Line of credit | Factoring |
|---|---|---|
| Based on | Your firm's revenue and history | Your clients' credit and invoices |
| Grows with billings | Not automatically | Yes |
| Best for | Short, repeating payroll gaps | Large invoices to big clients |
| Client sees it | No | Often, since the factor collects |

That last row matters. With many factoring arrangements, the client pays the factor directly. For some firms that's fine; for others, it affects a sensitive relationship. Ask how notification works.

## When a term loan is the better fit

For a one-time investment, like opening a second office, buying another firm's book of business or building a new practice area, a [Business Term Loan](/services/business-term-loan) ($10,000–$2,000,000, 6–36 months) gives you a fixed payment you can plan around. Don't use a revolving line for something you'll need years to pay back.

## Be careful with fast, expensive money

A [Merchant Cash Advance](/services/merchant-cash-advance) can fund quickly ($5,000–$5,000,000, 3–18 months), but it's costly, and its frequent fixed-rhythm payments work against the lumpy way services firms get paid. If you go that route, read the estimated APR disclosure New York requires for sales-based financing ([23 NYCRR 600.7](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/23-NYCRR-600.7)) and see our [factor rate guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-factor-rate).

## Using the line well

Once approved, a few habits keep the line healthy and help you qualify for more later:

- **Draw for specific gaps** with a known repayment date, like a client invoice due next month.
- **Repay as soon as the client pays**, rather than letting the balance roll.
- **Watch your utilization.** A line that stays fully drawn for months is a sign the gap has become structural, which may mean pricing, payment terms or staffing need to change.

## The short version

Lenders evaluate a services firm on deposits, receivables, client mix and existing debt. Get those in order, and a line of credit can carry payroll through slow-paying months.
