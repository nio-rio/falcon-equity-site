If your merchant cash advance is collected daily, you have three levers: extend the term, change the withholding, or replace several advances with one. **[Consolidation of Positions](/services/consolidation-of-positions)** is the third, and it is usually the one that moves the payment most, because the term stretches out over 6 to 36 months instead of 3 to 18. The catch is that a smaller payment over a longer term can still cost more in total, so the number worth agreeing on is the total payback, not only the daily debit.

## Why a daily remittance feels heavier than the rate suggests

A merchant cash advance is collected by remitting a share of your sales, often every business day. That structure has one advantage: you remit less when sales are slow. It also has one problem: the money leaves before you have decided what to do with it.

That is why owners describe the same situation in the same words — profitable on paper, short on cash. A profit and loss statement counts revenue when it is earned. Your bank account only sees what survived the remittance.

If that is your situation, you are not really shopping for a cheaper loan. You are looking for a payment schedule that matches how cash arrives.

## The three levers you actually have

1. **Stretch the term.** A longer repayment period is what turns a large daily debit into a smaller one. This is the lever consolidation pulls hardest.
2. **Change the withholding.** Some funders will renegotiate the percentage or the frequency. Ask early, while you are current, rather than after a debit fails.
3. **Consolidate several positions into one.** If more than one advance is running, the total of the remittances — not any single one — is what drains the account.

If you are holding two or more advances, start with the third lever. Our guide to [consolidating stacked advances](/blog/consolidation-of-positions-stacked-advances) covers how positions stack up in the first place.

## How consolidation lowers the payment

Consolidation of Positions replaces your open advances with a single new position. Three things change at once.

- **Several remittances become one payment.** Predictability matters as much as size when payroll and rent are queued behind an automatic pull.
- **The term stretches out.** Falcon Consolidation of Positions typically runs **$10,000 – $2,000,000 over 6 – 36 months**, against **3 – 18 months** on a merchant cash advance. The longer runway is what makes the payment smaller.
- **The old positions are paid off or restructured.** That is the part that stops the cycle of taking a new advance to cover an old one.

Typical consolidation files look for 6+ months in business, $15K+ monthly revenue and a 550+ credit score. Every file is reviewed individually, and approval is never guaranteed.

## What to ask before you consolidate

1. **What is the total payback in dollars?** Not the factor, not the daily payment — the total.
2. **What share of sales is remitted, and does it flex when sales drop?** A fixed daily debit and a percentage-of-sales remittance are very different promises.
3. **Which positions are being paid off, and will the liens be released?** Get the releases in writing as part of the close.
4. **How long is the term, and what happens if I pay it off early?** Ask whether early payoff reduces the cost or only ends the schedule.
5. **Does this payment work in my worst month, not my best?** Run the number against your slowest month of the last year.

## When a lower payment is the worse deal

Consolidation fixes a schedule. It does not fix a business.

- **The business still does not cover its costs after relief.** Stretching a shortfall over 36 months makes it larger, not smaller.
- **The new payment only works if revenue grows.** Relief that depends on a good month is not relief.
- **You are borrowing around tax debt you have not addressed.** Payroll tax problems get worse with time and need a tax professional first.
- **You have already consolidated once and fallen behind again.** A second consolidation on top of a failed first one rebuilds the stack.

## Red flags worth walking away from

- A fee taken before funding, or one explained only after you have signed.
- A quote that stays in factor-rate language and never states total payback in dollars.
- No review of your bank statements before a payment is quoted. Anyone pricing your file from a phone call is pricing it blind.
- Pressure to sign today.
- A promise to "make the old liens go away" without naming which positions are being paid.
