For a small manufacturer, a big new order can be the best and most stressful news of the year. Before you ship a single unit, you have to buy materials, schedule labor, maybe run overtime. Then you invoice, and the customer pays on net terms. Every step happens before you're paid.

That's why **working capital for manufacturers** is less about covering losses and more about funding a cycle. This guide covers the main options and how they fit together.

## Map your cash cycle

Start by measuring how long your money is tied up on a typical order:

1. **Materials lead time:** from paying suppliers to receiving materials.
2. **Production time:** from starting the run to finished goods.
3. **Shipping and acceptance:** until the customer accepts the delivery and you can invoice.
4. **Customer payment terms:** what the contract says, and what the customer actually does.

Add them up. That's how many days each dollar of materials and labor is out before it comes back. Multiply by your monthly production costs and you have a rough idea of how much working capital your business needs at current volume. A larger order or a slower customer increases it.

## Option 1: Invoice factoring

If your customers are distributors, retailers or other businesses that pay on terms, [invoice factoring](/services/invoice-factoring-tool) can shorten the last and often longest part of the cycle. You sell invoices to a factor, get most of their value soon after, and the factor collects from your customer.

- **Who it's for:** B2B businesses with invoices to creditworthy clients
- **Amounts:** $10,000–$3,000,000
- **Terms:** Ongoing facility
- **Requirements:** B2B invoices, creditworthy customers, 3+ months in business

Why it suits manufacturers:

- **It grows with sales.** More invoices mean more available funding.
- **It leans on your customers' credit.** Selling to a large, reliable buyer helps.
- **It doesn't add a fixed payment.** Your customer pays the invoice.

What it can't do: factor an order you haven't shipped. It funds the next run from the last one. For the difference between factoring and borrowing against receivables, see [invoice factoring vs. AR financing](/blog/invoice-factoring-vs-ar-financing).

## Option 2: Equipment financing for capacity

Sometimes the constraint isn't cash, it's machine time. A second CNC, a faster press, a packaging line or a forklift can let you take orders you'd otherwise turn away. Paying cash for that equipment pulls from the same working capital you need for materials.

[Collateral & Asset Loans](/services/collateral-asset-loans) finance the equipment over its working life:

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$1,000,000
- **Terms:** 12–60 months
- **Requirements:** an equipment quote or invoice, 6+ months in business, an active business

Ask the vendor to itemize installation, rigging, tooling and training. Our [equipment break-even guide](/blog/equipment-financing-break-even) shows how to test whether the added output covers the payment.

## Option 3: Term loan for a defined expansion

For a one-time project, like a new production line, a facility fit-out or a large opening order for a new customer, a [Business Term Loan](/services/business-term-loan) gives you a lump sum and a fixed schedule:

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$2,000,000
- **Terms:** 6–36 months
- **Requirements:** 6+ months in business, $15K+ monthly revenue, 550+ credit score

It's also a reasonable way to fund the first order from a new customer before you have invoices to factor.

## Option 4: Line of credit for materials

If you buy raw materials in repeating cycles, or want to buy ahead when prices are good, a [Business Line of Credit](/services/business-line-of-credit) gives you a revolving limit:

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$500,000
- **Terms:** Revolving
- **Requirements:** 6+ months in business, $10K+ monthly revenue, an active bank account

## How they fit together

| Need | Best-fit tool |
|---|---|
| Waiting on net-terms customers | Invoice factoring |
| Adding machine capacity | Equipment financing |
| One-time expansion or first big order | Term loan |
| Repeating material purchases | Line of credit |

Many manufacturers use two or three of these at once. If you plan to, tell your specialist up front. A factor typically files a claim on your receivables, and other lenders will see it, so the pieces need to be arranged to work together.

## Price slow payment into your quotes

If a customer pays late, financing that wait costs you money. Build it into pricing:

- **Know each customer's real payment time**, not just their stated terms.
- **Add the cost of carrying the invoice** to quotes for slow payers, or offer a small discount for early payment.
- **Ask for deposits** on large or custom orders where it's normal in your industry.
- **Watch concentration.** If one customer is most of your revenue, a delay from them affects everything. Factors and lenders look at this closely.

## Buying materials ahead of price changes

Material costs can move, and some manufacturers try to buy ahead when prices look favorable or when a supplier offers a volume discount. That can be smart, but it's also a bet. Before using financing to buy ahead, compare the expected saving with the cost of carrying the extra inventory: the financing cost, storage, insurance and the risk that specs or demand change before you use it. If the saving clearly outweighs those costs, a short draw on a line of credit is a reasonable way to fund it. If it's close, the safer choice is to buy what the current order book needs.

## Avoid short, expensive money for long needs

A [Merchant Cash Advance](/services/merchant-cash-advance) ($5,000–$5,000,000, 3–18 months) funds quickly, but it's expensive and its frequent repayments don't line up well with long production cycles. If you're already carrying several advances, [Consolidation of Positions](/services/consolidation-of-positions) can combine them and aims to cut current daily or weekly payments, in some cases by 30%–50%.

## Preparing your file

Helpful documents for a manufacturing application:

- Recent business bank statements, complete PDFs
- An accounts receivable aging report and sample invoices (for factoring)
- A customer list with typical order sizes and payment terms
- For equipment, an itemized vendor quote
- A list of existing loans, leases and advances

Factoring shortens the wait on customers. Equipment financing adds capacity without spending working capital. A term loan funds a one-time leap, and a line covers repeating material buys.
