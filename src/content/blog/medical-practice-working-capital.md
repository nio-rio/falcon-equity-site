A busy practice can still run out of cash. Patients are seen, claims go out, and then the practice waits while payers process, review and sometimes deny or request more information. Meanwhile, payroll is due every two weeks.

That's the core of **medical practice working capital**: funding the time between delivering care and getting paid for it. This guide covers the options and how to choose.

## Why practices run short

It's rarely a revenue problem. It's a timing problem, made worse by a few things that are specific to healthcare:

- **Claims take time.** Payers process claims on their own timelines, and any rework or denial adds more.
- **Payer mix matters.** Two practices with the same billings can have very different cash flow depending on who pays and how quickly.
- **New providers ramp slowly.** A clinician can be on payroll before every payer has finished credentialing them, which means salary before billable revenue.
- **Fixed overhead.** Staff, rent, malpractice insurance and software are paid regardless of what collected this month.

Knowing which of these drives your gap tells you which product fits.

## Option 1: A line of credit for the reimbursement cycle

For most practices, a revolving line is the best match. You draw when collections lag and repay as remittances land, then draw again when you need it.

Falcon's [Business Line of Credit](/services/business-line-of-credit):

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$500,000
- **Terms:** Revolving
- **Requirements:** 6+ months in business, $10K+ monthly revenue, an active bank account

A line works because the gap is repeating. You're not funding a one-time loss; you're smoothing a cycle. Apply while things are stable, not in the middle of a payer slowdown.

## Option 2: A term loan for a defined project

When the need is a project with a beginning and end, a [Business Term Loan](/services/business-term-loan) is usually cleaner:

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$2,000,000
- **Terms:** 6–36 months
- **Requirements:** 6+ months in business, $15K+ monthly revenue, 550+ credit score

Typical uses: building out a new operatory or exam room, opening a second location, or carrying a new provider through credentialing. You get a lump sum and a fixed payment you can plan around.

## Option 3: Equipment financing for clinical assets

Imaging systems, dental chairs, lasers and diagnostic devices last for years. Paying for them out of operating cash ties up the money you need for payroll. [Collateral & Asset Loans](/services/collateral-asset-loans) spread the cost over a term that matches the equipment:

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$1,000,000
- **Terms:** 12–60 months
- **Requirements:** an equipment quote or invoice, 6+ months in business, an active business

## What about factoring insurance claims?

Medical receivables factoring exists, but it's more complicated than factoring an ordinary business invoice:

- **Government payers.** Federal rules generally prohibit Medicare from paying amounts due a provider to anyone else under an assignment ([42 CFR 424.73](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/424.73)). Arrangements that work around this are more complex to set up.
- **Expected vs. billed.** A claim's value is what the payer will actually pay after adjustments, not what you billed, so advances are based on expected collections.
- **Privacy.** A third party handling claims will see patient information, which brings HIPAA obligations.

For a practice whose revenue is mostly commercial insurance with a clean billing history, factoring can be worth exploring. For many others, a line of credit is simpler. Ask your specialist which fits your payer mix.

## A word on merchant cash advances

A [Merchant Cash Advance](/services/merchant-cash-advance) can fund quickly ($5,000–$5,000,000, 3–18 months, 3+ months in business). For practices, though, it's often a poor fit for the reimbursement gap. It's expensive, and the frequent repayments come out of the same deposits you're waiting on, which can make the squeeze worse. Use it only for a short, specific need with a clear payback, and read the cost disclosure carefully. Our guide on [how to read a factor rate](/blog/how-to-read-a-factor-rate) helps.

## Shrink the gap, not just fund it

Financing is a bridge. The cheapest working capital is cash you collect sooner. Practices that tighten the revenue cycle need less financing:

- **Verify eligibility before the visit** to reduce denials.
- **Submit claims promptly** and track how long each payer actually takes.
- **Work denials quickly.** Old claims are harder to collect.
- **Collect patient responsibility at the time of service** where you can.

These are operational changes, but they directly reduce how much and how often you draw.

## Size the line to your real cycle

To decide how big a line you need, look at the last six to twelve months of collections by payer. Work out roughly how long each one takes to pay and how much is typically outstanding at any time. The amount you need to cover is the payroll, rent and supply costs that fall inside that window, minus the cash you keep on hand. Add a cushion for the months when a payer slows down or a batch of claims is denied and resubmitted. A line sized this way is used and repaid in a steady rhythm. A line sized to the maximum approval tends to drift upward and stay drawn, which costs more and leaves less room for a real surprise. Revisit the size once a year, or whenever you add a provider or a location.

## Preparing your file

Lenders mostly read your business bank statements, so they'll see insurance remittances, patient payments and existing debt payments. Before applying, gather:

- Recent business bank statements, complete PDFs
- Owner ID and practice details
- A list of any existing loans, leases or advances
- For equipment, the vendor quote

See [what your bank statements say](/blog/what-your-bank-statements-say) for what underwriters notice, and our [credit repair and loan prep guide](/resources/credit-repair-loan-prep) if your credit needs work first.
