For most independent retailers, the money that pays for December leaves the bank account in August or September. Orders are placed, deposits go to suppliers and freight gets paid long before a single holiday shopper walks in. That gap between buying and selling is what **retail inventory financing** is for.

Used well, it lets you stock the items customers come in for, take vendor discounts and avoid running out halfway through the season. Used badly, it leaves you with a payment schedule and a back room full of stock that didn't move. This guide covers how to pick the right type and how to size it.

## Start with the inventory, not the loan

Before you compare products, answer three questions about the stock itself:

1. **How fast will it sell?** Look at last year's sell-through for the same categories. Items that sell in four weeks need different financing from items that sit for four months.
2. **What is the margin?** The financing cost comes out of the margin. A low-margin item funded with expensive capital can sell out and still lose money.
3. **What happens to leftovers?** Can unsold stock carry into next season, be returned or only be marked down?

If you can answer these, you can work out how much to borrow and how long you need it for. The financing should match the life of the inventory, not the other way round.

## Your three main options

### 1. Business line of credit

A [Business Line of Credit](/services/business-line-of-credit) gives you a revolving limit you can draw on when you place an order and pay down as the stock sells. Once it's repaid, the limit is available again for the next season.

For a store that buys on a cycle, holiday, spring, back-to-school, this is usually the most efficient tool. You pay for what you draw, for as long as you have it drawn.

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$500,000
- **Terms:** Revolving
- **Requirements:** 6+ months in business, $10K+ monthly revenue, an active bank account

The catch is that you need some history to qualify, and a line has to be applied for *before* you need it. If you're reading this in October, it's late for this year and early for next.

### 2. Merchant cash advance

A [Merchant Cash Advance](/services/merchant-cash-advance) gives you a lump sum now in exchange for a share of future sales. Repayment comes out of your deposits, so it rises in busy weeks and falls in slow ones.

That flexibility and speed make it a real option for stores with heavy card sales and a short window, for example a fast-selling item that's about to stock out right before a holiday weekend.

- **Amounts:** $5,000–$5,000,000
- **Terms:** 3–18 months
- **Requirements:** 3+ months in business, $10K+ monthly revenue, an active business bank account

Be clear-eyed about cost. An MCA is priced with a factor rate rather than an interest rate, and when you convert it to an annual cost it is usually far more expensive than a line of credit or a term loan. New York requires providers of sales-based financing to show an estimated APR on the offer, so read that number before you sign ([23 NYCRR 600.7](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/23-NYCRR-600.7)). Our guide on [how to read a factor rate](/blog/how-to-read-a-factor-rate) walks through the math.

### 3. Business term loan

A [Business Term Loan](/services/business-term-loan) is a lump sum with fixed payments over a set period. It fits a one-time investment more than a seasonal cycle: stocking a second location, launching a new product category or buying a large opening order for a new brand.

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$2,000,000
- **Terms:** 6–36 months
- **Requirements:** 6+ months in business, $15K+ monthly revenue, 550+ credit score

The fixed payment keeps going after the holidays, so make sure January and February can carry it.

## How to size the order

Retailers get into trouble most often by borrowing to the approval limit rather than to the plan. A simple way to size it:

1. **List the order** by category with expected cost.
2. **Subtract what you can pay from cash** without dropping below your normal operating cushion.
3. **The difference is the amount to finance.**
4. **Estimate the payback window** from sell-through. If most of it should sell between mid-November and late December, you need financing for roughly that long, plus a buffer.
5. **Run the repayment** through the [loan calculator](/resources/loan-calculator) and check it against a slow-season month, not your best month.

If the numbers only work in the best case, order less.

## Plan for the slow-seller

Some of what you buy will sell slower than expected. Decide in advance:

- **Which items you'll mark down and when**, so cash comes back while the item still has value.
- **Whether a vendor will take returns** or offer credit for next season.
- **How you'll keep up repayment** if January is soft. With a line of credit, you can pay the balance down more slowly within the terms. With a term loan or advance, the schedule is set, so keep a reserve.

## Use the discount math

One place financing can pay for itself is vendor terms. If a supplier gives a discount for paying early or for ordering in volume, compare the dollars saved with the cost of the funds for the same period. If the saving is larger, the financing is earning its keep. If it's close, it's not worth the risk.

## Don't stack advances

If you already have one advance and take another to fund inventory, you end up with two daily or weekly payments coming out of the same deposits. This is called stacking, and it squeezes cash flow quickly. If that has already happened, [Consolidation of Positions](/services/consolidation-of-positions) can combine existing advances into one payment and aims to cut current daily or weekly payments, in some cases by 30%–50%. Our post on [stacked advances](/blog/consolidation-of-positions-stacked-advances) explains when it makes sense.

## Get your file ready before you need it

Underwriters mostly look at your bank statements. Clean, consistent deposits into a business account do more for an application than anything you can say about the season. Gather:

- The last several months of business bank statements (full PDFs)
- Business and owner details
- A list of any open advances or loans
- For fixtures or POS upgrades, a vendor quote

See the [funding checklist](/blog/24-48-hour-funding-checklist) for the full list, and [what your bank statements say](/blog/what-your-bank-statements-say) for what underwriters are looking for.

## The short version

For a repeating inventory cycle, a line of credit is usually the best fit. For a one-time push, a term loan. For speed on stock that will sell fast, an advance can work, if you've done the cost math.
