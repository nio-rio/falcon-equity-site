Take the offer only if the money earns more than it costs before the repayments come out of your sales. Stripe's own documentation says a Capital offer **might be a business loan or a merchant cash advance**, so the first question is which one you were given, and the second is the total payback in dollars. If you cannot answer both, you are not ready to accept.

## First: which product is this?

"Is Stripe Capital an MCA?" has a precise answer, and it is "sometimes". Stripe states that each offer specifies the type of financing, which might be a loan or a merchant cash advance, and that you cannot request a particular type. Where the offer is an MCA, Stripe describes it as its partner's purchase of your business's future receivables, collected as a share of your payment volume. Where it is a loan, it is a business-purpose term loan issued by a bank on a set schedule.

Shopify is more direct about structure: US funding is issued as loans by WebBank, repaid automatically as a fixed percentage of your daily sales, only on days you make sales, with a maximum repayment term of 18 months.

Neither product is open to everyone. Both are effectively closed to businesses that do not already process payments on that platform, which is the quiet catch in a "you're prequalified" dashboard message: the offer exists because they can see your sales, not because anyone has assessed your plans.

| | Stripe Capital | Shopify Capital |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Structure** | A term loan issued by a bank, or a merchant cash advance — the offer states which | Loans issued by WebBank in the US |
| **Cost** | Advance amount plus a flat fee; Stripe states there are no late, early-payment or origination fees | Shopify states no compounding interest; funding is repaid from sales |

## The six questions to answer before you accept

1. **What is the total payback in dollars?** A flat fee expressed as a multiplier hides the number that matters. Multiply the advance by the factor and write the total down. Our guide to [reading a factor rate](/blog/how-to-read-a-factor-rate) walks through the arithmetic.
2. **What is that as an annualized cost?** The same fee costs very different amounts depending on how fast it is collected. A percentage-of-sales hold takes longer in a slow quarter, which moves the effective annual cost.
3. **What share of sales is withheld, and does it step down when sales fall?** Repaying only on selling days is genuinely gentler than a fixed daily debit. Check whether the percentage itself is fixed even when volume collapses.
4. **What happens if sales do not come back?** For a loan product, find out what triggers a minimum payment and what happens when withholdings fall short of it.
5. **Does this sit on top of financing I already have?** If you are already repaying an advance, a new one stacks on it. Add up every withdrawal before adding another.
6. **What does this money buy, and what does that return?** Write down the specific use and the expected return. "Cash flow" is not a use.

## When an offer like this makes sense

- **A known return inside the repayment window.** Inventory for a line that reliably sells through, or ad spend with a measured return, can earn more than the flat fee costs.
- **Seasonal timing.** Money that buys stock ahead of a peak, repaid from the peak's own sales, is the structure these products were built for.
- **Speed where the amount is modest.** If the alternative is losing a supplier or missing a launch window, the cost of waiting can exceed the cost of the fee.

## When it does not

- **Conversion is down and you are buying inventory anyway.** More stock does not fix a demand problem. It converts a revenue problem into a storage and debt problem.
- **You are covering a loss, not funding a return.** Financing a shortfall with a fixed fee makes the shortfall bigger.
- **You already have an advance running.** This is how a stack gets built: each new offer looks affordable on its own and impossible in aggregate. If that is already you, read [how consolidation works](/blog/consolidation-of-positions-stacked-advances).
- **You cannot state the total payback from memory.** If it is not clear enough to recall, it is not clear enough to accept.

## Where a revolving line fits instead

The difference between a line of credit and an advance is when the cost starts. An advance starts costing you the moment it lands, whether or not the money is put to work. A **[business line of credit](/services/business-line-of-credit)** is drawn when you need it and repaid as cash comes in, so the cost tracks the actual gap rather than a calendar. Falcon lines of credit typically run $10,000 – $500,000, revolving, for businesses with 6+ months in business, $10K+ monthly revenue and an active bank account.

A **[merchant cash advance](/services/merchant-cash-advance)** remains the better fit for a one-time buy with a clear return: Falcon advances typically run $5,000 – $5,000,000 over 3 – 18 months, for businesses 3+ months in operation with $10K+ monthly revenue, with a 0% agreement fee.

Which one fits depends on the shape of the need, not on which offer arrived first.

## The bottom line

An offer in your dashboard is a price, not an endorsement. Get total payback in dollars, convert it to an annualized cost, check what happens in your worst month, and write down what the money buys. If those four answers look good, the offer probably is.
