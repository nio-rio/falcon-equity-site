Ask ten carriers how they fund their business and you'll hear "factoring" from most of them. Ask the same carriers how they bought their last truck and you'll hear "a loan." Both answers are right, because they solve different problems.

This guide explains **trucking factoring** and trucking business loans side by side so you can match the tool to the problem in front of you.

## Two different problems

Before comparing products, decide which problem you actually have.

**A timing problem.** You hauled the load, the invoice is out, and the money is owed to you, but brokers and shippers pay on their schedule while fuel, insurance and drivers are paid on yours. The money exists; it just hasn't arrived.

**A capital problem.** You need something you don't have yet: another tractor, a reefer trailer, a cash reserve for a new lane. The money doesn't exist until you borrow it.

Factoring solves timing problems. Loans solve capital problems. Using the wrong one costs more or simply doesn't work.

## How freight factoring works

With factoring, you sell your invoices for delivered loads to a factoring company. The factor advances most of the invoice value soon after you submit it, collects from your customer, and then pays you the remainder minus its fee.

What that means in practice:

- **No fixed payment schedule.** You aren't repaying principal on a calendar. The customer pays the invoice.
- **It grows with your freight.** More delivered loads mean more invoices you can factor.
- **Approval leans on your customers.** The factor cares most about whether your brokers and shippers pay. That's why newer carriers with good customers often qualify for factoring before they'd qualify for a bank loan.
- **It only works on earned revenue.** You can't factor a load you haven't delivered.

### What factoring costs

Factoring fees are charged per invoice and often rise the longer the customer takes to pay. The headline rate is only part of the picture. When you compare offers, line up:

- The fee for an invoice paid in your customers' typical time
- The advance percentage (how much you get up front)
- Any extra fees: per-transfer charges, monthly minimums, early termination
- **Recourse vs. non-recourse:** with recourse, you owe the factor if your customer doesn't pay
- Whether you must factor every invoice or can choose

A cheap-looking rate with a high monthly minimum can cost more than a slightly higher rate with none.

## How a trucking business loan works

A loan gives you a lump sum or a credit limit that you repay on a set schedule. For carriers, two types matter most.

### Equipment financing for trucks and trailers

[Collateral & Asset Loans](/services/collateral-asset-loans) fund a specific piece of equipment, which also secures the financing.

- **For:** tractors, trailers, reefers, liftgates and other equipment
- **Amounts:** $10,000–$1,000,000
- **Terms:** 12–60 months
- **Requirements:** an equipment quote or invoice, 6+ months in business, an active business

The key test is whether the asset earns more than its payment. A truck on a steady lane that clears its monthly payment with room to spare is a sound purchase. A truck bought on hope is not. Our [equipment break-even guide](/blog/equipment-financing-break-even) shows how to run that number.

### A line of credit for repairs and renewals

A [Business Line of Credit](/services/business-line-of-credit) is useful for costs that don't come with an invoice to factor: a major repair, an insurance down payment, permits.

- **Amounts:** $10,000–$500,000
- **Terms:** Revolving
- **Requirements:** 6+ months in business, $10K+ monthly revenue, an active bank account

## Side by side

| | Factoring | Equipment loan | Line of credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solves | Waiting on payment | Buying an asset | Irregular costs |
| Approval leans on | Your customers | You and the asset | You and your revenue |
| Repayment | Customer pays the invoice | Fixed schedule | Repay what you draw |
| Grows with freight | Yes | No | Not automatically |
| Can fund a truck | No | Yes | Small purchases only |

## When carriers use both

The most common setup for a growing fleet is a combination:

- **Equipment financing** on each truck or trailer, so the asset pays for itself over its working life.
- **Factoring** on receivables, so fuel and payroll aren't held hostage to broker payment terms.
- **A line of credit** as a backstop for repairs and renewals.

One caution: factors usually file a claim on your receivables, and other lenders will see it. If you plan to add a loan later, tell your specialist up front so the pieces fit together.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Borrowing to plug a timing gap.** If the money is already owed to you, paying interest to cover the wait is usually the more expensive route.
- **Using short-term advances to buy trucks.** Short, expensive money for a long-lived asset strains cash flow every week.
- **Ignoring customer concentration.** If one broker is most of your revenue, a factor will look closely at that broker. Diversifying helps both your business and your terms.
- **Signing without reading exit terms.** Know how to leave a factoring agreement before you sign one.

## What to have ready

Whichever route you take, a complete file moves faster. For factoring, have your operating authority details, a list of the brokers and shippers you haul for, recent invoices with signed rate confirmations and proof of delivery, and your business bank statements. For equipment financing, add the dealer's quote or invoice for the specific unit, with year, make, model and VIN if it's used. If you already factor with another company, bring that agreement too, because a new factor or lender will need to know how it ends. The [funding checklist](/blog/24-48-hour-funding-checklist) covers the general documents most applications need.

If your problem is "they owe me and haven't paid," look at factoring. If it's "I need a truck," look at equipment financing. If it's both, you may need both.
