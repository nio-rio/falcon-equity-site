Before an underwriter reads a word of your application, they read your bank statements. Three things decide most of the conversation: whether deposits arrive consistently, whether payments bounce, and whether you hold a balance at the end of the month. Everything in the application is either confirmation or explanation.

## Why the statements come first

An application tells underwriters what you say about your business. Six months of statements tell them what the business actually did. The gap between those two accounts is where declines come from — not usually from dishonesty, but from owners describing their best month as if it were typical.

Statements are hard to argue with: a deposit history shows a pattern or it does not. That is why they are read first.

## The three lines underwriters look at

### 1. Deposit consistency

What matters is not the size of the biggest month, but whether money arrives on a rhythm. A business depositing $40,000–$55,000 a month for six months reads as stable. A business with three months at $18,000 and one at $90,000 reads as unpredictable, even if the annual total is the same.

Underwriters look for:

- **Regular business deposits**, not one large transfer propping up a slow month
- **Recurring customers or repeat card volume**, which suggests demand rather than a single event
- **Seasonality that repeats**, which is fine and explainable
- **A trend line.** Three consecutive declining months raise a different question than a dip followed by recovery.

### 2. Returned payments and negative balances

Every returned item is visible, including the ones that were covered the same afternoon. A handful over six months is normal for many businesses and usually explainable. A cluster — three in one month, or anything near the application date — needs an explanation before it becomes a question.

Negative balances matter for the same reason. They show how close to the edge the account runs, and an account that touches zero most weeks is being managed on timing rather than margin.

### 3. Ending balances

Ending balances are the quiet test. They show what is left after everything has cleared — the actual cushion the business operates with. Underwriters are not looking for a large balance; they are looking for a balance that does not disappear.

## What else gets noticed

- **Transfers between accounts.** Often legitimate, but they can make revenue look larger than it is. If you move money between accounts, say so.
- **Unusual deposits.** A loan, a tax refund, a sale of an asset, an owner contribution — label them. An unexplained $30,000 deposit is a question.
- **Payments to other funders.** Open positions are visible whether or not they are disclosed. Undisclosed positions are one of the fastest routes to a decline, and the most common.

## What weak statements look like

None of these is a decline on its own. Together they explain most difficult files.

- Deposits that swing widely with no seasonal logic
- Several returned payments in the most recent two months
- Ending balances near zero across all six months
- Payments to multiple funders, none of them disclosed
- Revenue that only exists because of inter-account transfers

## How to present your file well

1. **Six full months, every page.** Download the PDFs directly from your bank. Screenshots, exports with missing pages and cropped statements all slow the file down.
2. **Every account that receives deposits.** A second account that takes card settlements is part of your revenue; leaving it out looks like a gap.
3. **A one-page note on anything unusual.** A slow quarter, a customer who left, a machine that was down for three weeks. Written before it is asked, it reads as management. Written after, it reads as an excuse.
4. **Disclose every open position.** Funder, balance, payment amount and frequency. This is the single item most often omitted and most easily detected.
5. **Keep it current.** Statements older than the last completed month raise a question about what has changed since.

## What not to do

- **Do not move money around to improve the picture.** Deposits that appear and leave within days are obvious, and they damage credibility far more than a thin balance would.
- **Do not take another advance immediately before applying.** The new payment shows up, and the file becomes about the stack.
- **Do not omit an account or a position.** Both are visible in the statements you submit.
- **Do not send edited documents.** A PDF that has been altered is usually spotted, and it ends the conversation.

## If your statements are weak

Weak statements are a reason to change the ask or the product, not necessarily a reason to stop. A smaller amount, a different structure, or a short waiting period after fixing a returned-payment pattern can all move a file forward. What does not work is applying repeatedly with the same statements and expecting a different answer.
